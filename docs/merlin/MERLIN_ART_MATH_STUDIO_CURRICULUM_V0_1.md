# Merlin Art × Mathematics Studio: research-backed curriculum architecture v0.1

**Status:** research synthesis and curriculum specification. This is not yet a deployed course platform or evidence that the proposed sequence improves outcomes.

## Executive recommendation

Build a **dual-discipline studio-and-inquiry curriculum**. Mathematics must not be reduced to a tool for making attractive pictures; art must not be reduced to decoration for mathematical exercises. Each project must have two independently assessable outcomes:

1. a mathematical outcome: observe, represent, calculate, model, generalize, prove or test;
2. an artistic outcome: compose, make, interpret, critique, revise or communicate.

The common unit is a personally meaningful artifact plus an explicit account of the decisions and structures that produced it.

Use the recurring cycle:

**Notice → Wonder → Sketch → Make → Measure → Formalize → Critique → Transform → Prove/Test → Exhibit → Reflect → Revisit.**

Move repeatedly between physical materials, hand drawing, mathematical notation, dynamic geometry, code and finished artifacts. Do not assume that digital is always better: a 2024 meta-analysis of 41 visualization interventions (10,562 learners) found a medium overall positive effect on mathematics outcomes (g = 0.504), with positive lasting effects, but technology-based visualization was not superior to analog visualization. This supports modality choice based on the learning goal, not novelty.

## Research synthesis and design implications

### 1. Visualization is a serious mathematical learning method

Schoenherr, Strohmaier and Schukajlow (2024), *Learning with visualizations helps*, synthesized 41 studies and 10,562 learners. The pooled effect was medium (g = 0.504; 95% CI 0.379–0.630), with meaningful heterogeneity. Implication: use diagrams, gesture, physical construction, sketches and dynamic representations, then require learners to explain what the representation reveals and what it hides.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2024.100639

### 2. Integration needs deliberate boundary crossing

Potari and Choutou (2024) studied collaboration between secondary mathematics and visual-art teachers. The boundaries included disciplinary practices, tools and curricula; analytical and visual thinking did not automatically integrate. Facilitation, shared artifacts and coordination mattered. Implication: every Merlin project needs a mathematics thread, an art thread and a designed bridge between them. A teacher/agent should explicitly translate between the two modes.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmathb.2024.101138

### 3. Make original work, not only analyze finished work

Nutov (2021) studied integration of visual arts into mathematics teacher education. Its reported findings included positive associations between art that illustrates mathematical concepts and mathematical achievement, with original artwork creation associated with higher scores than analysis of existing artwork in the study context. These results are not a universal causal guarantee. Implication: learners should create, revise and explain their own work, alongside careful analysis of existing works.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tate.2020.103218

### 4. Aesthetic experience and constructionism belong in mathematics

Karavakou, Kynigos and Sinclair (2023) describe mathematics-as-artistic, aesthetically rich mathematical experiences, and constructionism: learners make personally meaningful digital artifacts while developing mathematical sense-making. Implication: allow open-ended choices and multiple valid artifacts, but anchor reflection in precise mathematical questions, conjectures and tests.

Source: https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2023.1284718

### 5. Visual art can connect to broad STEM and design practice

Zhang and Jia (2024) review visual-art STEAM examples and discuss observation, description, comparison, causal reasoning, critical thinking and design-based/problem-based learning. Implication: use genuine making processes—draft, prototype, material constraints, critique and iteration—not just a final image.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1007/s44217-024-00354-w

### 6. Function-based generative art is a useful advanced bridge

Bautista (2025) studied students creating function-based artwork with GeoGebra. Reported strategies included grouping functions by image parts, reflection and symmetry, combining animation with transformations and using multiple tools. Implication: use parameterized artwork to teach functions, transformations, composition and the consequences of changing a rule.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1111/ssm.18373

### Evidence limitations

These studies differ in population, design, duration and outcome measures. They do not establish one universally optimal sequence, prove that every art integration improves mathematics, or validate Merlin's adaptive engine. Merlin must run its own pre/post, delayed-retention and transfer evaluations. Treat the architecture below as a research-informed design hypothesis.

## The curriculum: six connected studios

The six studios are not age grades. Learners enter by demonstrated capability and can move between studios. Each has beginner, intermediate, advanced and research-level branches.

### Studio A — Seeing, drawing and measuring

**Art practices:** observational drawing, line, shape, negative space, value, color, visual description, composition, printmaking and material exploration.

**Mathematics:** measurement, ratio, fractions, coordinate systems, Euclidean geometry, scale, vectors, transformations, symmetry and geometric construction.

**Signature investigations:**
- Draw an object from observation; compare proportions and negative spaces.
- Create a composition under a constraint such as a fixed grid, ratio or set of shapes.
- Construct a motif using translation, rotation and reflection.
- Compare freehand, ruler/compass and coordinate-based constructions.

**Advanced extension:** projective geometry, affine transformations, perspective, calibration, error propagation and computer vision.

### Studio B — Pattern, symmetry and ornament

**Art practices:** pattern design, textile structures, mosaics, tiling, printmaking, ornament, calligraphy/letterform studies and visual rhythm.

**Mathematics:** congruence, similarity, Euclidean isometries, cyclic/dihedral groups, wallpaper groups, modular arithmetic, combinatorics and symmetry breaking.

**Signature investigations:**
- Create a repeat unit, then classify its symmetry.
- Test which polygons tile the plane under declared constraints.
- Compare a hand-drawn pattern with a generated one.
- Alter one symmetry rule and explain the visual and group-theoretic consequences.

**Advanced extension:** crystallographic groups, group actions, quotient spaces, substitution tilings, aperiodic tilings and symmetry on surfaces.

**Cultural practice:** study specific works, communities, makers and historical contexts. Never imply that a culture is reducible to a mathematical motif or that a pattern's meaning can be inferred from geometry alone. Credit sources and distinguish historical attribution from mathematical analysis.

### Studio C — Space, sculpture and architecture

**Art practices:** sculpture, origami, model-making, installation, architecture, spatial composition and material studies.

**Mathematics:** 3D geometry, polyhedra, topology, coordinate transforms, vectors, matrices, trigonometry, differential geometry, optimization and structural constraints.

**Signature investigations:**
- Fold a 2D net into a 3D form and prove which edges meet.
- Build a structure under material and load constraints.
- Photograph a sculpture from multiple viewpoints and reconstruct its projections.
- Compare intrinsic surface properties with the appearance of a projection.

**Advanced extension:** curvature, geodesics, manifolds, topology, finite-element modeling, computational geometry and fabrication constraints.

### Studio D — Motion, time, sound and light

**Art practices:** animation, dance notation, film, photography, sound art, music visualization, kinetic sculpture and light installation.

**Mathematics:** functions, sequences, trigonometry, complex numbers, Fourier analysis, differential equations, probability, signal processing, rotations and dynamical systems.

**Signature investigations:**
- Animate one shape through a family of transformations.
- Translate rhythm into a time series and then into visual composition.
- Compare periodic motion with non-periodic motion.
- Make a light/shadow study and infer geometric constraints.

**Advanced extension:** Fourier series/transforms, wavelets, nonlinear dynamics, phase spaces, stochastic processes and spacetime geometry.

### Studio E — Generative, computational and data art

**Art practices:** generative drawing, procedural design, digital collage, data visualization, interactive media and algorithmic composition.

**Mathematics:** functions, recursion, fractals, graph theory, probability, statistics, information theory, linear algebra, optimization, computational geometry and machine learning.

**Signature investigations:**
- Write a small rule that generates a visual family.
- Change one parameter and predict how the artwork changes.
- Create a fractal and compare mathematical self-similarity with perceived visual texture.
- Turn a dataset into an artwork and disclose transformations, missingness and uncertainty.

**Advanced extension:** generative grammars, cellular automata, stochastic geometry, topology, neural representations, differentiable rendering and computational creativity.

### Studio F — Mathematical art research atelier

**Art practices:** independent studio practice, critical writing, exhibition, curatorial research, collaboration and public explanation.

**Mathematics:** proof, abstraction, mathematical modeling, research methods, formal systems, category/representation ideas, topology, geometry, analysis, algebra, probability and computation.

**Signature investigations:**
- Formulate an original question arising from an artifact.
- Define objects, assumptions and invariants.
- Construct examples and counterexamples.
- Compare competing representations and identify information lost by each.
- Formalize a precise claim, test it computationally and prove it where feasible.
- Present an artwork, research notebook, reproducible code and a critical account of limitations.

**Research-level outputs:** a mathematically characterized art series, a new generative method with proofs or bounds, a computational design paper, a visual explanation of a theorem, or a documented artistic investigation that generates a precise mathematical conjecture.

## The teaching engine: a dual objective, not a blended score

For every lesson, maintain separate evidence for:

### Mathematical competencies
- perception and pattern recognition;
- accurate construction and measurement;
- representation translation;
- quantitative reasoning and estimation;
- abstraction and generalization;
- proof, counterexample and logical justification;
- modeling, computation and uncertainty;
- transfer to unfamiliar objects or domains.

### Artistic competencies
- observation and visual analysis;
- intention and concept development;
- composition and formal decisions;
- technique and material control;
- originality and meaningful variation;
- critique and interpretation;
- iteration and revision;
- communication and contextual understanding.

Do not infer mathematical mastery from an attractive image or artistic mastery from a correct formula. A learner may be strong in one dimension and developing in another. Report a profile, not a single opaque grade.

## The canonical lesson protocol

1. **Encounter:** show an artwork, object, movement, pattern, material or visual puzzle.
2. **Notice:** learner records observations without being told the target theorem.
3. **Wonder:** learner poses questions and makes a prediction.
4. **Make:** create a sketch or physical artifact before the full formal explanation.
5. **Manipulate:** vary one controlled feature; compare the before/after.
6. **Name:** introduce the mathematical language that describes the observed structure.
7. **Translate:** express the same object in at least two representations (drawing, coordinates, equation, code, construction, verbal account).
8. **Justify:** derive a rule, prove a property or identify the limits of a conjecture.
9. **Critique:** evaluate artistic intent and mathematical correctness separately.
10. **Revise:** change the artifact based on both kinds of feedback.
11. **Transfer:** solve a new problem that changes the medium, context or representation.
12. **Revisit:** delayed retrieval with a fresh artifact or counterexample.

Use worked examples and graduated hints for novices; fade scaffolding as competence grows. Never turn every open artistic decision into a fixed-answer quiz.

## Merlin learner measurement

### Evidence event schema

Each attempt should store:
- pseudonymous learner and session IDs;
- idea ID, art-skill ID, problem ID, lesson and version;
- task medium and representation;
- rubric version and assessor provenance;
- response/artifact reference and integrity hash where applicable;
- scores for each mathematical and artistic dimension;
- confidence and self-explanation;
- hint level, revisions and time-on-task (only when meaningful);
- transfer/delayed flags;
- reviewer feedback and adjudication state.

Store artifact content in an appropriately scoped artifact store; keep event metadata and evidence references in the learner ledger. Do not place private student work in public repositories.

### Competency inference

- Maintain a vector of estimates for each competency, not one global mastery score.
- Track observation count, task difficulty, independent sessions, uncertainty, evidence age and transfer.
- A correct multiple-choice answer is weak evidence by itself.
- Require varied tasks, constructed explanation and transfer before marking a skill provisionally mastered.
- Require delayed retrieval and independent work for retention.
- Use human moderation for portfolio quality and contextual interpretation.
- Calibrate rubrics and item difficulty against expert ratings and held-out learner cohorts.
- Check accessibility and fairness; do not infer intelligence, creativity or potential from speed, style, disability, language accent or tool choice.

### Hermes routing

Hermes should choose the next activity by:
1. learner's chosen artistic interests and accessible modalities;
2. prerequisite readiness in both the mathematical and artistic graphs;
3. the most consequential gap or uncertainty;
4. whether a new medium could reveal a misconception;
5. the opportunity for transfer across representations;
6. spacing and evidence age;
7. course/teacher objectives.

A lesson may be recommended only when its prerequisite policy is satisfied, unless it is explicitly labeled an exploratory preview. Return a reason trace: which evidence supported the recommendation, which prerequisites are ready, what remains uncertain and why this task is next. Recommendations never grant execution authority or determine certification automatically.

## Assessment: portfolio plus independent problem solving

Use a balanced evidence set:
- **Observe:** describe an unfamiliar artifact accurately.
- **Reconstruct:** recreate its structure from partial information.
- **Generate:** create a new artifact under stated constraints.
- **Translate:** express the same structure in a different representation.
- **Explain:** describe choices and mathematical relationships.
- **Prove/test:** justify a claim or find a counterexample.
- **Critique:** evaluate artistic meaning and formal correctness separately.
- **Transfer:** apply the idea in a new medium or domain.
- **Reflect:** identify what changed in the learner's own understanding.

A portfolio is evidence, not a substitute for independent problem-solving. A test is evidence, not a substitute for making and interpretation.

## The first 12-project sequence

1. **One shape, many transformations:** draw, rotate, reflect, translate; introduce isometries.
2. **A pattern that repeats:** design a repeat unit; classify cyclic/dihedral symmetry.
3. **Tessellation studio:** construct a plane-filling pattern and justify the tiling.
4. **Perspective as projection:** make a one-point perspective drawing; derive the projection constraints.
5. **Proportion and composition:** compare ratios and framing; critique the golden-ratio myth as well as genuine proportional choices.
6. **Sculpture from a net:** build a polyhedron; study faces, edges, vertices and duality.
7. **Curves that move:** design parametric curves; study functions, derivatives and curvature.
8. **Fractal garden:** iterate a rule; investigate recursion, dimension and scale.
9. **Sound becomes image:** map rhythm and frequency into visual form; study periodicity and Fourier ideas.
10. **Data portrait:** create a data artwork; study distributions, uncertainty, encoding and ethical interpretation.
11. **Algorithmic atelier:** create a generative artwork; study algorithms, randomness, constraints and reproducibility.
12. **Independent research exhibition:** learner poses a question, creates an artifact, formalizes the mathematics and defends the result.

## Highest-level extension: the research apprenticeship

After the 12 projects, learners select a research thread and work with a human mentor plus Merlin's specialist agents:
- geometry and symmetry;
- topology and knot/textile structures;
- architecture and differential geometry;
- generative art and dynamical systems;
- color, perception and mathematical imaging;
- sound, music and harmonic analysis;
- data art and statistical ethics;
- mathematical visualization and proof communication;
- computational creativity and human/AI authorship.

Every research project must include a literature map, explicit question, definitions, assumptions, examples, counterexamples, artifact, code/data provenance, mathematical argument, independent replication where applicable and a statement of what remains unknown. Lean can verify suitable formal statements; numerical tests and simulations are evidence of bounded behavior, not proofs of universal claims.

## AI and generative-art policy

Use generative AI as an object of study, optional tool and critique target—not as a replacement for learner agency.
- Learners disclose which parts are human-made, model-generated, edited or algorithmically transformed.
- Compare prompt-only output with learner-directed iteration and hand-made alternatives.
- Evaluate the model's visual claims and mathematical structure rather than trusting the image.
- Preserve provenance and respect licensing, attribution, privacy and cultural context.
- Never score creativity solely by novelty metrics or similarity to a model's preferred style.
- Teach dataset bias, representation limits, copyright/consent questions and the distinction between producing an image and understanding its construction.

## Acceptance criteria for Merlin

- Every studio has explicit mathematical and artistic competencies.
- Every project maps to canonical mathematical ideas and versioned art-skill IDs.
- Lessons support physical, hand-drawn and digital pathways.
- Each learner has a multidimensional, uncertainty-aware evidence profile.
- Hermes recommendations include prerequisite and evidence reason traces.
- Assessment includes making, explanation, formal reasoning, critique and transfer.
- Aesthetic quality and mathematical correctness are evaluated independently.
- The system can recommend enrichment to advanced learners without forcing everyone through a single grade ladder.
- Research-level claims are provenance-linked and qualified by proof, replication or clearly labeled evidence.
- Student artifacts and identities remain private and tenant-scoped.
